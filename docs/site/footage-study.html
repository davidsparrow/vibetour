Footage Study

Date 12 Oct 2026 Status bake-off rounds 1 and 2 done Standard draft 0.1, changes queued for 0.2 Spend so far $13

Verdict

Keep the plates clean, put the realism in a cockpit layer. The reference video's feeling of driving comes from the cockpit framing and the light, not from steering. Baking hands and a dashboard into 45 to 150 generated clips can't stay consistent, and it leaves nowhere for the live gauges, mirrors and lights that make VibeTour react to the IDE.

HeyGen Video is real, cheap and useful, but not for anchored clips. It takes a first frame only. Its jobs here: trailers and previews, and possibly cruise plates if a looser join style passes the bake-off.

Round 1 of the bake-off is in. Seedance 2.0 Fast made a true anchored loop (seam 0.96) at full speed. Veo 3.1 Lite drove well and landed on a lookalike of its anchor for a third of the price. Kling crawled. HeyGen's two takes are clean drives at $0.135 each, usable with dissolve joins only. The cockpit layer composited over the Veo loop on the first try. Round 2 decides the default join style and the model.

What the reference actually does

Frames pulled at 2 fps across the 5 min 53 s sample show three things the eye misses at speed:

  1. The hands don't steer. The wheel and hands are static in every clip while the world scrolls. On the canyon and desert stretches there is no wheel rotation across 6 s.
  2. It's a montage. Clips of 5 to 8 s, hard cuts, and a different car in almost every one: a Ferrari, a Mercedes, a Porsche, a bare wheel with gloves and no hands.
  3. What sells it is the framing (wheel, A-pillars, rear-view mirror, dash under the glass) and the light (sun flare across the dash, reflections in the glass, the night-city glow on the gauges).

That's fine for a music channel. It's exactly what a Journey Pack can't have: one interior, one pair of hands and one light across every clip, playing for 90 minutes.

Why anchors? And do we need them?

The standard asks every clip to start and end on a shared frame so the player can chain them with no visible join. The question from the review: why not play 10 s of one stretch, dissolve, and jump to another stretch of the same road?

Anchored joins

  • Invisible joins, true loops for holds.
  • Needs first-and-last-frame models: Veo 3.1, Kling 3.0, Hailuo 3, Seedance 2, Wan 2.7, FLUX.3.
  • Retakes: a clip that misses its anchor is rejected.

Dissolve joins

  • A 1 s dissolve between any two clips of the same scene. Works with any model, including HeyGen at a third of the price.
  • About 400 dissolves in a 90-minute session. In peripheral vision that may read as a flicker, or as a pleasant drift.
  • Holds still loop: crossfade the tail into the head offline, which works on gentle motion.

The camera rules stay either way: same lane, same light, same sun direction, or a dissolve between sun-left and sun-right looks wrong. The cockpit layer makes dissolves easier to accept, because the cockpit never changes; only the view beyond the glass does.

Proposal: make anchors a join style in the manifest ("joins": "anchored" | "dissolve") rather than a requirement, and let the bake-off decide the default. "Anchored" could earn a marketplace badge.

The cockpit layer

Plate

The windshield footage, as the standard already specifies: no car, anchors, loops.

made by: the pack, any model
Cockpit

A still of dash, A-pillars, mirror housing and roofline with the windshield cut to alpha, plus wheel and hands as a separate sublayer that rotates about the hub.

made by: an image model, once per car
Instruments

Gauges, mirrors with crew agents, rain on the glass, the your-turn light, the refuel stop. Everything VibeTour draws today.

made by: existing code

What makes it feel alive

  1. Steering that matches the road. Each clip carries a small steering track, a few degrees over time, derived from the plate's optical flow at build time. Gentle curves need ±10 to 15°, which a 2.5D wheel-and-hands cutout sells. Big turns don't exist; the standard forbids them. At stops the wheel centres and a "hands resting" still crossfades in.
  2. Light from the plate onto the dash. A blurred, screen-blended copy of the plate over the cockpit for reflections, a tint from the variant's time of day, a sun flare positioned from the plate. This is most of what the reference's best frames are doing.
  3. Micro-motion. ±1 to 2° of sway and a small bob keyed to speed. Nothing else.

Why it beats baked-in footage

  • Cars become a product axis. "Pick your car" alongside "pick your journey": a neutral modern interior, a classic convertible, an EV with a glass dash, a fantasy sports car with no badge. One still each, works with every pack, swappable in Settings.
  • Consistency is structural, not hoped for across 150 generations.
  • Cost. Plates stay cheap; the cockpit is one image, not 20 minutes of retakes.
  • The standard's camera rules need one change: the plate's vanishing point moves up a little, because the cockpit covers more of the bottom than the current 30% band.

Baked cockpit footage still has a place: the pack trailer and marketplace preview, made with HeyGen reference-to-video from the same cockpit still. Cuts are a feature there, and it's cents per shot.

The cockpit composite

What the composite taught us, for draft 0.2:

  • The cockpit covers the lower 45% of the frame, not 30%. Plates for a cockpit layer need the vanishing point around a quarter from the top and a wider lens, or the dash hides the road. The proof of concept zooms the plate 1.6× to compensate, which production footage shouldn't need.
  • Despill after keying, with no expansion, or the dash takes a magenta cast.
  • The validator must crop letterbox bars and match scale before scoring frames against anchors; raw SSIM against the anchor read 0.80 even for a first frame that was the anchor.

What round 2 settles

  • No single model wins every slot. Seedance owns the stop set; Veo owns cruise and transitions at a third of the price. The "one model per pack" rule in the draft should relax to "one model per slot family", with the look matched through the anchors, which all models honour closely (first frames score 0.83–0.91 against their anchors).
  • Stop-in needs a nearer anchor. Asking a model to arrive at a different place in 8 s produces morphs. The stop anchor should be an edit of the road anchor with the stop already in view, and the pull-in should be 10 to 12 s.
  • Transitions want 12 s or more, or a feature that hides the join, as the draft already suggests.
  • The dissolve path has a rule of its own: each HeyGen shot must chain from the last frame of the shot before it, or stops and holds end up in different places. That makes it a chain, not a graph, and it can't loop. It still fits trailers and previews.
  • Steering works as a 2.5D layer. A wheel-and-hands cutout, taken from the difference between the cockpit still and an edit of it with the wheel removed, rotates ±12.5° about the hub convincingly. The cockpit needs a taller canvas so the arms run out of frame, or the rotated cut shows. Finger re-grips and a tap on the wheel are later experiments.

Two 60-second composited sessions, one per join style, sit in the review folder beside the clips: session-anchored-seedance.mp4 and session-dissolve-heygen.mp4. Judged on those, dissolve won. That removes the last-frame requirement from the standard entirely: anchors become look references, holds loop by crossfade, and HeyGen at $0.017/s joins the cruise-plate options. Clips should run 12 to 15 s so a session has half as many dissolves.

Cockpit v2: mirrors, steering from the road, glare

ChangeHow
FramingThe cockpit is generated 4:3, square to the car, wheel hub at the centre, and the 16:9 frame is cropped from the top, so the forearms run out of the bottom and the rotated cut never shows. (A first attempt with the wheel at 40% turned the whole cabin to the right; see "Aim" below.)
Wheel and handsAn image-model edit removes the wheel and hands; the difference between the two stills, clipped to a disc round the hub and two arm bands, is the cutout. The cutout is keyed and despilled like the cockpit, which removed a green fleck on the rim.
Steering follows the roadDense optical flow on the plate at 320×180; the median horizontal flow in the band round the horizon is the camera's yaw. Smoothed over half a second, scaled so the 97th percentile is 12.5°, and sent to the rotate filter frame by frame. On the Big Sur loop the wheel rises to 11.8° at the apex and settles to 3° where the road is still bending, instead of returning to centre.
MirrorsThe glass is found as the transparent regions of the keyed still that aren't the windshield (two components, 235×90 and 99×82). What a mirror shows is the road receding, so the feed is the same plate run backwards at 320×180, darkened 20%: the centre for the rear-view, the left edge for the door mirror.
GlareThe plate's highlights above 150 are blurred and screened back onto the glass region only, so bloom sits where the sun is and moves with it. For stronger flare the plates should be prompted for a low sun in frame; HeyGen and Seedance both oblige.

Everything above runs from two generated stills per car ($0.14) and one FFmpeg graph. The session renders at about real time on a laptop.

Aim: the cockpit must be generated straight ahead

The v2 render pointed the car about 45° to the right, and it took a side-by-side with the v1 session to see why. Asking the image model to put the wheel at 40% of the frame didn't slide the camera sideways; it turned the camera toward the passenger side: the left A-pillar landed in the centre of the frame and the dash ran away to the right. No plate offset can fix a turned cabin. The rule for the standard, in two parts: generate the cockpit square to the car, wheel hub at the horizontal centre, an A-pillar at each edge; and keep the plate centred so its vanishing point sits over the hub. An off-centre framing, if ever wanted, comes from cropping a wider straight-ahead still, never from the prompt. Cockpit v3 is that straight-ahead still, on the tall canvas so the arms still run out of frame.

Sun and flare

Two layers, and both were needed. The plate is prompted for a low sun in frame with bloom, a streak and ghost spots, and both Veo and HeyGen render that convincingly from an anchor that already has it. Then a flare layer (flare.py) finds the sun each frame as one round, clipping blob in the sky (cloud banks are irregular and are ignored), and draws a bloom, an anamorphic streak and five tinted ghost spots on the line through the frame centre, with the intensity eased so it never flickers. It's confined to the glass, with faint ghosts allowed across the dash and none on the headliner or the mirror housing. It's a per-variant feature: on for "into the sun" variants, off otherwise. Left on over a midday plate it fired on the clouds and tinted the whole cabin green, which is how v4's colour problem was found.

Cockpit v4: colour, light and materials

The review said it plainly: beige, washed out, a dash like a block of clay, dials you could barely see. Three causes, all self-inflicted:

  • The prompts asked for it. Every anchor and clip said "golden hour, natural muted colour, no HDR look", advice lifted from HeyGen's docs for talking-head work. The reference footage is the opposite: high sun, polarised air, saturated blues and greens. The vivid anchor prompt names the colours it wants ("deep cerulean sky, cobalt to turquoise water, trees in several distinct greens") and what it doesn't ("not beige, not muted, not golden-hour"), and both Veo and HeyGen follow it from the first frame.
  • The cockpit prompt asked for charcoal. v4 names the materials: tan saddle leather with stitching, open-pore walnut, brushed aluminium, gloss black, a perforated leather wheel with a metal spoke insert, and a backlit cluster with white numerals and glowing needles, lit by daylight from the windshield. The model delivers all of it in one still.
  • The composite added haze. A warm blurred copy of the plate was screened over the dash at 40%. It's now neutral (luminance only) at 15%, the bloom only takes near-white highlights and is desaturated, and the plate gets a light grade (+8% saturation, +4% contrast) rather than a heavy one.

The standard's anchor and clip prompt templates will carry the vivid wording, and the cockpit template will list materials and the lit cluster. Per-variant palettes (midday, golden hour, night city with a glowing red cluster like the reference) become a creator choice, not an accident of the prompt.

The vivid session: waves, guardrail, the whole journey

A new master anchor for Big Sur carries the two details from the reference footage: the corrugated galvanised guardrail on weathered wooden posts along the cliff edge, and swells crashing onto the rocks with spray exploding upward. The other three anchors (Carmel, the gas-station approach, the stop) were made from it as a style reference so the light and palette hold across the pack. The clip prompts describe the waves as an event ("every few seconds a wave hits and white spray explodes upward and hangs in the air") rather than as scenery, and both Veo and HeyGen animate it.

A note on logos

Using a real badge without permission isn't free advertising in the law's eyes; it's trademark use, and a marketplace that sells packs makes it commercial use. Ferrari in particular polices this. Unbranded interiors cost nothing now. If a real car matters later, treat it as a partnership to ask for, not a default.
